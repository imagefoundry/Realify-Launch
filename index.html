Before you
send anything

Nothing moves but the quality. Here are five things that decide whether a view works, so you know exactly what you're getting before you commit to it.

Everything a buyer or a planner could hold you to stays. Building form and massing, roof pitch and line, window and door positions and sizes, material type and where each material sits on the elevation, boundaries, levels and the camera itself.

What changes is the part that was never specified. Light, sky and weather, which is the point of the exercise. Ground texture. The exact species and density of planting. Background vehicles and distant context.

Your image is rebuilt rather than adjusted. That's what lets it read as photography instead of a graded render. It's also why it comes back in days rather than weeks. Send your material schedule and your specification is held to the real references.

Low-res files are the main reason a view doesn't work. There isn't enough information in the file, so more of the detail has to be rebuilt. The further it can move from your specification. If a file is borderline you'll hear that from us before you pay.

We work to a fixed set of frames, so if your view doesn't already sit in one of them it needs cropping to fit. That's a decision about your image, so you should make it rather than us. Tell us at the outset if you need the same view in more than one ratio.

Tell us at the outset. Planting generally stays where it is. Most of the time nobody minds whether a shrub is exactly that shrub. Sometimes it matters a great deal. We won't know which unless you say.

Flag anything fixed, including:

  • A specific tree or plant, either the species or its exact position. Retained trees under a TPO are the common one.
  • A named material, given as the real reference rather than the render's version of it.
  • Specific hard landscaping. A bin store, a bike shelter, a substation, a paving type, an access route that has to read clearly.
  • A boundary treatment that was a condition rather than a choice.
  • Anything already approved against a planning condition, or already printed for a sales suite.
  • Anything that has to match a sibling image in the same set.

If you flagged it and we've missed it, the fix is on us.

Individual house types and small groups work best. One building, one camera, a clear foreground.

Street scenes and masterplans are harder. There's more competing detail in one frame and more materials working against each other. A street where every plot shares the same brick and roof is workable. Four different palettes across eight plots isn't, at this stage. Send it over and you'll get a straight answer on whether it's worth doing.

You'll also need the right to have your image adapted. Commissioning a CGI doesn't always carry that, so it's worth a check with whoever produced it.
